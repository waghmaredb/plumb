# Make Compliance the Gate That Sells

The compliance and standards layer should not be an appendix to the engagement; it should be the mechanism that decides which of the firm's recommendations survive, the artefact the client reuses as audit evidence, and the credential that lets a small Singapore firm sell into a market the government is deliberately seeding. Four design decisions follow from the evidence. First, score compliance maturity as a ninth dimension built from evidence held rather than certificates claimed, because the gap between self-reported audit-readiness (**74%** in Schellman's 2026 survey) and the existence of a formal AI policy (**38%** in ISACA's 3,400-respondent poll) shows that survey-based scoring will flatter almost every client ([Schellman](https://www.schellman.com/blog/ai-governance/why-organizations-arent-audit-ready), [ISACA](https://www.isaca.org/about-us/newsroom/press-releases/2026/ai-use-accelerates-while-governance-and-roi-lag-says-new-isaca-research)). Second, attach a five-tier risk classification with rule-based overrides and per-tier controls to every value pool and use case, with agents gated on reversibility, because every published scheme from Canada's AIA to MAS's October 2026 guidelines converges on that shape. Third, structure deliverables as the ISO/IEC 42001 clause documents, the EU Annex IV-style technical file and the MAS inventory record, because those are the exact items auditors sample first and the ones GRC platforms still cannot automate. Fourth, build and attest the firm's own research tool to SOC 2 and ISO 27001 before ISO 42001, treat citation verification as a formal Processing Integrity control, and pursue IMDA's AI Tester Accreditation Programme, which accredited its first five testers on 14 October 2026 ([LittleBigRedDot](https://littlebigreddot.com/imda-ai-agent-testing-kit-accredited-ai-testers-fairs-2026/)). The regulatory map as of 25 October 2026 is thinner than the noise suggests: binding, dated obligations for APJC enterprises are MAS's guidelines effective 7 October 2027 and 2028, Australia's automated-decision transparency rule from 10 December 2026, Korea's AI Basic Act in force since 22 January 2026 with fines deferred, Vietnam's AI law transitioning to March and September 2027, China's labelling and conduct regimes, India's DPDP penalties from 13 May 2027, and the EU's Article 50 duties already live with high-risk obligations pushed to 2 December 2027 by the Digital Omnibus ([Baker McKenzie](https://www.bakermckenzie.com/en/insight/publications/2026/10/singapore-mas-issues-guidelines-on-artificial-intelligence-risk-management), [NatLawReview](https://natlawreview.com/article/eu-digital-omnibus-ai-enters-force)). Everything else, including every ISO and NIST artefact, is voluntary or draft, and regulators are converging on disclosure, contractual rights and in-context testing rather than naming certifications. The evidence that compliance correlates with value is consistent but entirely correlational: responsible-AI leaders report roughly twice the business benefits, APAC firms with mature governance show about 5% higher revenue growth, and shadow AI adds US$670,000 to a breach ([BCG](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2022/a-responsible-ai-leader-does-more-than-just-avoiding-risk), [Deloitte APAC](https://www.deloitte.com/cn/en/about/press-room/apac-trustworthy-ai-report.html), [IBM](https://www.ibm.com/think/x-force/2025-cost-of-a-data-breach-navigating-ai)). The failure cases are more persuasive than the surveys: Deloitte Australia refunded part of an A$440,000 report over fabricated citations and triggered new procurement disclosure clauses within three months, which is the precise failure mode of the firm's own product ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/10/07/deloitte-ai-australia-government-report-hallucinations-technology-290000-refund)).

## Only a thin band of AI rules binds APJC clients before 2027

The most important fact for calibrating the compliance layer is how little is actually binding today, and how precisely dated the things that will bind are. Singapore has no omnibus AI statute and no plan for one, governing through the PDPA, sector regulators and a dense stack of voluntary IMDA and AI Verify frameworks ([Chambers](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/artificial-intelligence-2026/singapore)). The decisive 2026 change is MAS's Guidelines on AI Risk Management, issued 7 October 2026 after a consultation that closed 31 January 2026: board and senior-management oversight and the AI inventory, materiality and policy framework apply from **7 October 2027**, and lifecycle controls, third-party AI and capability requirements from **7 October 2028**, covering all financial institutions and explicitly including generative AI and agents ([Baker McKenzie](https://www.bakermckenzie.com/en/insight/publications/2026/10/singapore-mas-issues-guidelines-on-artificial-intelligence-risk-management), [Allen & Gledhill](https://www.allenandgledhill.com/sg/publication/articles/34686/mas-publishes-ai-risk-management-guidelines-setting-out-supervisory-expectations-on-responsible-ai-adoption)). The consultation text sets the risk-materiality dimensions as Impact, Complexity and Reliance, requires formal independent validation before deployment and periodic re-validation for high-materiality AI, and specifies the inventory fields (purpose, approved scope, model type, data, dependencies, lifecycle status, materiality rating, validation status, roles, documentation links) that any deliverable for an FI client should populate ([MAS consultation PDF](https://www.mas.gov.sg/-/media/mas-media-library/publications/consultations/bd/2025/final_consultation_paper_on_guidelines_on_ai_risk_management_forrelease.pdf)). The final text, per practitioner commentary that should be verified against the MAS PDF, expects independent evidence for third-party models rather than contractual representations, agent identifiers and tool-access records, pre-deployment red-teaming and regularly tested kill switches ([Luke Soon](https://lukesoon.substack.com/p/mas-finalises-ai-risk-guidelines)). Around MAS sit the PDPA (penalty cap the greater of S$1 million or 10% of local turnover), the PDPC's 2024 advisory guidelines on AI recommendation and decision systems, MOH/HSA's AIHGle 2.0 of 10 March 2026, CSA's Securing Agentic AI addendum of 17 June 2026, and IMDA's Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI of 22 January 2026, updated in May ([CMS](https://cms.law/en/sgp/legal-updates/Organizations-face-risk-of-higher-financial-penalties-for-breaches-of-the-PDPA-from-1-October-2022), [Baker McKenzie](https://www.bakermckenzie.com/en/insight/publications/2026/07/singapore-csas-addendum-on-securing-agentic-ai-systems), [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/04/singapores-agentic-ai-framework-practical-guidance-for-market-entry)).

The rest of the region splits into three postures. Korea is the only North-East Asian jurisdiction where a deployer can be fined: the AI Basic Act has been in force since 22 January 2026 with extraterritorial reach, high-impact domains that include hiring, loan screening, healthcare and biometrics, a domestic-representative requirement at revenue above KRW 1 trillion or one million daily Korean users, and fines capped at KRW 30 million but deferred for at least a year ([Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-27-south-koreas-ai-basic-act-overview-and-key-takeaways)). Vietnam's Law 134/2025/QH15 is the only binding horizontal AI law in ASEAN, in force 1 March 2026 with transitions to 1 March 2027 generally and 1 September 2027 for healthcare, education and finance, pre-market conformity assessment for listed high-risk systems and portal registration ([CASRAI](https://casrai.org/guides/vietnam-ai-law-134-2025)). China's binding stack keeps thickening: mandatory labelling under GB 45438-2025 since 1 September 2025, an amended Cybersecurity Law from 1 January 2026 with fines up to RMB 10 million, and anthropomorphic-interaction measures effective 15 July 2026 ([Covington](https://www.covingtonblogs.com/2025/03/18/china-releases-new-labeling-requirements-for-ai-generated-content/), [Latham & Watkins](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/chinas-cybersecurity-law-amendments-increase-penalties-broaden-extraterritorial-enforcement)). India chose no new AI law but has two binding instruments: the synthetic-media amendment to the IT Rules effective 20 February 2026 with mandatory labels and a three-hour takedown, and the DPDP Rules whose penalties of up to INR 250 crore bite from 13 May 2027 ([Hogan Lovells](https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/india-introduces-mandatory-labelling-for-ai-and-3hour-takedown-for-illegal-content), [Uniqus](https://uniqus.com/digital-personal-data-protection-act-timelines/)). Australia is the most volatile: it dropped ten mandatory guardrails in the December 2025 National AI Plan, then announced legislated "Australian Standards for AI" on 15 July 2026 with a bill expected early 2027 and nothing yet tabled, while the only live rules are the DTA policy for government AI (v2.0, with a 30 April 2027 milestone) and the Privacy Act's automated-decision transparency duty commencing **10 December 2026** ([Compliance Council](https://compliancecouncil.com.au/insights/australia-ai-standards-july-2026-reversal-what-it-means/), [WAItech](https://waitech.com.au/blog-details/ai-reporting-obligations-regulated-industries-australia-2026)). Japan, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and the Philippines are principles-and-plans regimes where exposure runs through sector regulators and privacy law; Hong Kong's SFC circular of 12 November 2024 is the sharpest of these, treating any generative AI touching investment advice as high-risk and requiring human review, robustness testing and SFC notification ([Clifford Chance](https://www.cliffordchance.com/insights/resources/blogs/talking-tech/en/articles/2024/11/sfc-circular-to-licensed-corporations-on-use-of-generative-ai.html)). Malaysia's AI Governance Bill (consultation closed 31 July 2026) and Thailand's revised draft AI Act (consultation closed 14 August 2026, fines THB 1–5 million) both aim for passage by end-2026 with EU-style tiers, which is why a tiering scheme built now will port across the region ([Baker McKenzie](https://www.bakermckenzie.com/en/insight/publications/2026/07/malaysia-public-consultation-on-the-ai-governance-bill), [Tilleke & Gibbins](https://www.tilleke.com/insights/thailand-releases-new-draft-artificial-intelligence-act/)).

Outside the region, the EU AI Act after Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (in force 27 July 2026) has prohibitions live since February 2025, GPAI duties since 2 August 2025, Article 50 transparency duties since 2 August 2026 with machine-readable marking of legacy generative systems due by 2 December 2026, Annex III high-risk obligations moved to **2 December 2027** and Annex I to 2 August 2028, with penalties unchanged at €35 million or 7%, €15 million or 3%, and €7.5 million or 1% ([White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/eu-ai-omnibus-enters-force-amending-ai-act), [AI Act Explorer](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/99/)). The only harmonised standard is EN 18286:2026, cited in the Official Journal on 14 October 2026; ISO/IEC 42001 confers no presumption of conformity, and the Commission's stated position is that 42001's goals and definitions are not aligned with the Act's QMS requirement ([Nemko](https://digital.nemko.com/news/en-18286-eu-ai-act-cites-new-harmonized-qms-standard), [Stepto](https://stepto.net/blog/iso-42001-ai-vendor-assurance-procurement-gate-2026)). The United States has no federal statute, a deregulatory executive posture that has not yet preempted any state law, and a state patchwork in which Texas TRAIGA and California's ADMT regulations took effect 1 January 2026, Colorado's narrowed SB 26-189 applies from 1 January 2027, and the FTC's "Operation AI Comply" has brought more than a dozen AI-washing cases ([Glacis](https://www.glacis.io/guide-state-ai-laws), [Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/operation-ai-comply-2-years-later-continued-enforcement)). Federal procurement under OMB M-25-21 and M-25-22 demands testing rights, monitoring, and IP and data-ownership terms, and names neither NIST AI RMF nor ISO 42001 ([Centre Law Group](https://centrelawgroup.com/blog/current-omb-requirements-for-federal-ai-use-and-procurement/)).

The standards side is similarly stratified by status. ISO/IEC 42001:2023 remains first edition with no amendment registered; 42005 (impact assessment) and 42006 (certification-body requirements) are published, ISO/IEC 27090 on AI security threats was published 14 October 2026, while 42007, 42003, 42105 (human oversight, at FDIS) and 27091 are drafts ([GAICC](https://gaicc.org/blog/iso-42000-standards-family/), [Orbit/Reconn](https://orbit.reconn.io/iso-iec-27090-2026-ai-security-guide/)). NIST AI RMF 1.0 and the AI 600-1 generative profile are stable, but the RMF is officially under revision following the July 2025 AI Action Plan with no draft published, and the Cyber AI Profile (IR 8596), the SP 800-53 overlays (renamed COSIS on 20 October 2026) and the AI Agent Standards Initiative are all preliminary ([NIST AIRC](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/0-ai-rmf-1-0), [NIST CSRC](https://csrc.nist.gov/Projects/cosais)). The AICPA has issued no AI-specific trust services criteria; its only AI artefact is the nonauthoritative TQA 9561 of 11 September 2026 ([Arthur AI](https://www.arthur.ai/column/soc-2-ai-agents-aicpa-tqa-9561)). OWASP's LLM Top 10 2026 (released August 2026, with Excessive Agency rising to third) and the Agentic Top 10 (ASI01–ASI10, December 2025) are current; MITRE ATLAS now releases monthly and its agentic-tagged entries (139) outnumber predictive ones ([Superwise](https://superwise.ai/learn/owasp-top-10-for-llm-applications/), [Speakeasy](https://www.speakeasy.com/blog/mitre-atlas-explained)). ## Compliance maturity correlates with value, and liability lands on the deployer

Every major survey finds the same direction of association, and none of them claims causation. BCG and MIT Sloan's 2022 study reported responsible-AI leaders **twice as likely** as non-leaders to realise business benefits, with 50% versus 19% reporting better products and nearly 30% fewer AI failures where responsible AI was scaled before AI itself ([BCG](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2022/a-responsible-ai-leader-does-more-than-just-avoiding-risk)). Deloitte's Q2 2024 survey of 1,982 respondents found "trust builders" 18 points more likely to sit in the top third for achieving expected benefits, while "risk ready" respondents, who invested in risk controls without trust-building actions, were 16 points less likely than average to achieve higher benefits, and trust and risk actions were only weakly correlated (r = 0.204) ([Deloitte Insights](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/topics/risk-management/trust-in-ai.html)). That single finding is the strongest argument for the positioning this report recommends: compliance framed purely as control does not correlate with value, compliance framed as trust-building does. Deloitte's APAC survey of around 900 leaders found **91%** of organisations rated "basic" or "in progress" on governance, with mature governance associated with 28% more staff using AI and roughly 5% higher revenue growth, and two in five lacking an AI incident reporting mechanism ([Deloitte APAC](https://www.deloitte.com/cn/en/about/press-room/apac-trustworthy-ai-report.html)). McKinsey's 2026 trust-maturity survey puts average maturity at 2.3 on a 0–4 scale, finds organisations with explicit responsible-AI ownership averaging 2.6 against 1.8 without, and reports that firms investing US$25 million or more are far more likely to report EBIT impact above 5%, while naming APAC the leading region ([McKinsey 2026](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/tech-and-ai/our-insights/tech-forward/state-of-ai-trust-in-2026-shifting-to-the-agentic-era)). Accenture and Stanford's 2024 study found 0% of 1,000 companies at the top "Pioneer" stage, respondents estimating that a major AI incident would erase 24% of market cap, and firms spending more than 10% of their AI budget on responsible AI twice as likely to reach operational maturity ([Accenture PDF](https://www.accenture.com/content/dam/accenture/final/accenture-com/document-3/Accenture-Responsible-AI-From-Compliance-To-Confidence-Report.pdf)). On the cost side, IBM's 2025 Cost of a Data Breach found 63% of organisations with no AI governance policy and high shadow AI adding **US$670,000** to the average breach ([IBM](https://www.ibm.com/think/x-force/2025-cost-of-a-data-breach-navigating-ai)). Gartner expects more than 40% of agentic AI projects to be cancelled by end-2027, with inadequate risk controls among the named causes ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027)). No independent longitudinal study links responsible-AI maturity to financial performance; the deliverable should cite these as survey correlations with year and sample, and the value-axis of the maturity score should never treat a compliance score as a proxy for realised EBIT.

The enforcement record is more instructive than the surveys because it shows where liability actually settles. The Deloitte Australia case is the direct precedent for the firm's product. A report worth just under A$440,000 for the Department of Employment and Workplace Relations, published July 2025, contained a dozen references to a non-existent professor's work, a wrong Robodebt citation and a fabricated judicial quote; a University of Sydney academic surfaced the errors, Deloitte repaid the final instalment, the corrected version disclosed an Azure OpenAI GPT-4o toolchain, the Department of Finance reviewed Deloitte's supplier listing, a Senate committee questioned Deloitte and KPMG on 3 December 2025, and the APS AI Plan of 12 November 2025 introduced RFQ-stage AI-use disclosure and a clause making providers fully responsible regardless of generative AI use ([ACS](https://ia.acs.org.au/article/2025/deloitte-to-refund-government-over-ai-errors.html), [Bloomberg Tax](https://news.bloombergtax.com/daily-tax-report/australian-lawmakers-question-deloitte-and-kpmg-on-ai-misuse), [Canberra Times](https://www.canberratimes.com.au/story/9110516/new-ai-plan-for-the-aps-sets-out-expectations-for-contractors/)). Air Canada's 2024 tribunal loss established that a company is responsible for what its chatbot says even when it argues the bot is a separate entity ([ABA](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2024-february/bc-tribunal-confirms-companies-remain-liable-information-provided-ai-chatbot/)). Mobley v. Workday has let a vendor-as-agent theory proceed, conditionally certified a nationwide age-discrimination collective in May 2025, and produced a representation that Workday's tools were used to reject **1.1 billion** applications in the period, which makes employment screening a mandatory top-tier use case regardless of score ([AI Standard of Care](https://aistandardofcare.com/resources/mobley-workday-class-action/)). Regulators are acting on data-residency and legal-basis grounds: Italy's Garante blocked DeepSeek on 31 January 2025 and Korea's PIPC suspended downloads after finding prompt content transferred without consent to a ByteDance affiliate ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2025/01/31/deepseek-ai-blocked-by-italian-authorities-as-others-member-states-open-probes), [Bitdefender](https://www.bitdefender.com/en-us/blog/hotforsecurity/deepseek-ai-returns-to-south-korea-despite-past-privacy-violations)). But enforcement is contestable: the Garante's €15 million OpenAI fine was annulled in full by the Court of Rome on 18 March 2026 on lead-authority grounds, which means where an LLM vendor's EU establishment sits is itself a vendor-selection factor ([Gage Academy](https://www.gage.academy/tools/eu-ai-act-enforcement-tracker/actions/AIT-2024-0007-garante-openai-fine-annulled-court-of-rome)). The older cases set the ceiling for public-sector automation: Robodebt produced 470,000 wrongly issued debts, an A$1.872 billion settlement and 57 Royal Commission recommendations including independent expert scrutiny of business rules and algorithms, and the Dutch childcare-benefits scandal brought down a cabinet ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robodebt_scheme), [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/dutch-dpa-fines-tax-authority-2-75m-euros)). Across all of these the pattern is identical: neither "the model did it" nor "the vendor did it" holds, and the deploying organisation, or the consultancy that delivered the report, carries the loss. No MAS enforcement action specifically for AI model risk was found, which is consistent with Singapore's supervisory rather than punitive posture.

## Score compliance as evidence held, not certificates claimed

The earlier methodology placed governance and responsible AI as one of eight maturity dimensions. The compliance layer should both deepen that dimension and add a separate regulatory-exposure map, keeping the two distinct because one measures what the client has built and the other measures what the world will demand of them. Three design rules follow from the evidence. First, score from evidence, not survey: Schellman found 74% of US leaders say they could pass an AI compliance audit today while only 57% have a formal policy, 44% have documented AI incident response and 12% consider themselves ready for APAC AI regulation ([Schellman](https://www.schellman.com/blog/ai-governance/why-organizations-arent-audit-ready)); ISACA found 56% do not know how long it would take to halt an AI system after an incident and 39% do not know whether a documented shutdown process exists ([ISACA](https://www.isaca.org/about-us/newsroom/press-releases/2026/ai-use-accelerates-while-governance-and-roi-lag-says-new-isaca-research)). Second, separate "certification held" from "contractual and testing evidence available", because regulators and government buyers are converging on disclosure, contract rights and in-context testing rather than named certifications, and MAS expects FIs to test third-party AI with their own data rather than rely on vendor attestations ([Allen & Gledhill](https://www.allenandgledhill.com/sg/publication/articles/34686/mas-publishes-ai-risk-management-guidelines-setting-out-supervisory-expectations-on-responsible-ai-adoption)). Third, borrow the item banks that already exist rather than inventing a taxonomy: ISO 42001's 38 Annex A controls across nine areas, NIST AI RMF's 72 subcategories, AI Verify's 106 process checks across 11 principles, OWASP's governance maturity levels 0–4 and adoption tiers AT0–AT8, and the EY Confidence Index pattern of Low/Medium/High per principle weighted by business relevance ([iso42k.com](https://www.iso42k.com/), [AI Verify Foundation](https://aiverifyfoundation.sg/what-is-ai-verify/), [CSA note](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-owasp-agentic-ai-governance-maturity-v2-20/), [EY PDF](https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/en-ca/services/ai/documents/ey-ai-confidence-index-discussion-paper.pdf)). Every published Big 4 and MBB rubric shares principle-level dimensions, a four-level scale and a risk tier that drives review cadence, and none publishes its scoring, so mirroring that structure infringes nothing and publishing the anchors differentiates.

The compliance-maturity dimension should carry eight sub-dimensions, each scored 0–4 against behavioural anchors with a required evidence class and a weight that rises with the client's regulatory exposure. The sub-dimensions are governance and accountability (named accountable executive, AI policy reviewed at least annually, board reporting, which ISACA finds at 38% board confidence), inventory and materiality (a use-case-level inventory with MAS fields and risk tiers), impact and risk assessment (an ISO 6.1.4 or 42005-style assessment, a DPIA where personal data is present, and for EU-exposed systems a fundamental-rights assessment), lifecycle controls (data governance and provenance, pre-deployment testing, validation independence scaled to materiality, change management, decommissioning), third-party and model-supply chain (vendor evidence beyond SOC 2, in-context testing, contractual visibility into model changes, concentration risk measured as the count of material use cases on one provider), security and adversarial resilience (OWASP LLM 2026 and ASI coverage, ATLAS-informed red-teaming, prompt-injection controls per input channel), transparency and human oversight (AI-interaction disclosure, synthetic-content marking, override logging and automation-bias monitoring, tested kill switches), and monitoring and incident response (drift thresholds, KRIs, incident reporting with timelines, six-month decision reconstruction through model, prompt and retrieval-corpus versioning, which CoreLine reports buyers now ask for) ([CoreLine](https://coreline.agency/blog/ai-governance-enterprise-procurement/)). Level anchors should follow OWASP's governance ladder: level 0 unaware, level 1 experimentation without guardrails, level 2 published policy with mandatory human review for high-impact decisions and a named executive, level 3 real-time anomaly detection with working kill switches and machine-readable policy, level 4 cryptographic agent identity and tamper-evident audit trails ([CSA note](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-owasp-agentic-ai-governance-maturity-v2-20/)). Calibration should assume most clients land at level 1–2: McKinsey's global average is 2.3 on its scale and only about a third reach level 3 on governance and agentic controls ([McKinsey 2026](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/tech-and-ai/our-insights/tech-forward/state-of-ai-trust-in-2026-shifting-to-the-agentic-era)). Credit for a vendor's SOC 2 Type II should be awarded only where the system description explicitly scopes model lineage, inference logging, drift monitoring and AI subprocessors, because CSA documents that two AI vendors can hold clean SOC 2 reports while testing entirely different control sets ([CSA](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-soc2-ai-controls-gap-20260830-csa-styled/)). An ISO 42001 certificate should score as governance evidence with a note on accreditation status, since several 2024 certificates were issued before certification bodies were accredited and 42006 only published in July 2025 ([Atoro](https://atoro.io/iso-42001-certification-bodies/)). Few APJC peers will hold one: of 223 publicly announced certificates, 65% are US-headquartered, with India at 11, Australia at 6 and China at 4 ([ISO42k](https://www.iso42k.com/state-of-iso-42001)).

The regulatory-exposure map is a separate matrix: jurisdictions the client operates or sells into, crossed with the use-case domains the engagement proposes, populated with the binding instrument, the date it binds, the penalty ceiling and the control it forces. For a Singapore FI with Australian and Hong Kong subsidiaries considering credit-decisioning and advisory copilots, the map shows MAS AIRG at October 2027 and 2028, APP 1.7–1.9 at 10 December 2026, the SFC circular already live for anything touching investment advice, and PDPA transfer limitations under section 26 for any offshore model route ([DeepInspect](https://www.deepinspect.ai/blog/singapore-pdpa-ai-controls-mapping)). The map should carry three status colours, binding, dated-but-not-yet-binding and voluntary-or-draft, and should be regenerated at each benchmark refresh because the Australian, Malaysian and Thai positions can move within a quarter. Benchmark context matters here too: Schellman reports 94% of US enterprises already operate under some AI regulation while only 29% rate themselves EU AI Act ready, and framework influence on decisions runs GDPR 60%, EU AI Act 43%, ISO 42001 36%, NIST AI RMF 33%, so a DPIA-first workflow remains the practical spine onto which ISO 42001's impact assessment and 42005 can be layered rather than run separately ([Schellman](https://www.schellman.com/blog/ai-governance/why-organizations-arent-audit-ready), [CoreLine](https://coreline.agency/blog/ai-governance-enterprise-procurement/)).

## Tier every value pool and use case, and gate agents by reversibility

Published tiering schemes converge on three to five tiers keyed to impact on people, data sensitivity, autonomy and reversibility, with rule-based overrides that force a higher tier regardless of score. Canada's Algorithmic Impact Assessment scores 65 risk questions (169 points) and 41 mitigation questions, deducts 15% where mitigation exceeds 80% of maximum, bins into Levels I–IV at 25-point quartiles, requires the assessment at design, before production and on scope change, and scales obligations by level: fully automated decisions permitted only at Levels I–II, specific human intervention points and a human final decision at III–IV, one expert review at II rising to two independent reviews plus publication at IV, and approved contingency plans before operation at IV ([Canada.ca](https://www.canada.ca/en/government/system/digital-government/digital-government-innovations/responsible-use-ai/algorithmic-impact-assessment.html), [AI Governance Academy](https://www.aiacademy.guru/governance/learn/operations/impact-assessments/canada-aia-deep-dive/)). MAS's Impact, Complexity and Reliance dimensions drive independent validation and contingency plans for high materiality and permit peer review for lower materiality, with explainability more exacting for credit decisioning and insurance underwriting ([MAS PDF](https://www.mas.gov.sg/-/media/mas-media-library/publications/consultations/bd/2025/final_consultation_paper_on_guidelines_on_ai_risk_management_forrelease.pdf)). A representative enterprise GRC template scores data sensitivity, decision impact, autonomy and external exposure 0–3 each, bands the total into Low (0–5), Moderate (6–12), High (13–20) and Critical (21+), overrides for employment decisions, regulated eligibility, sensitive data at scale and autonomous production actions, and records every decision as approve, condition, reject or escalate ([SmartSuite](https://www.smartsuite.com/grc-foundation/ai-governance/how-to-classify-ai-use-cases-by-risk-tier)). EY's Confidence Index uses the tier to set review cycles of every three, two or one years and monitoring yearly, quarterly or monthly ([EY PDF](https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/en-ca/services/ai/documents/ey-ai-confidence-index-discussion-paper.pdf)). KPMG's controls guide describes tiering at intake reviewed by an AI governance committee, with high-risk systems carrying a documented QMS and automated post-deployment monitoring ([KPMG PDF](https://kpmg.com/kpmg-us/content/dam/kpmg/pdf/2025/trusted-ai-controls-matrix-tool-us.pdf)). Credit decisioning and HR screening land in the top tier in every scheme reviewed, including EU Annex III, MAS, Korea's high-impact list and the Mobley litigation, so the tool should hard-code them as mandatory escalation.

The recommended gate therefore scores five criteria 0–3 (people impact and consequentiality, data sensitivity, autonomy and reliance, external exposure, reversibility of actions) at intake, applies overrides, and assigns one of five tiers. Each value pool inherits the highest tier of the use cases inside it, and the value-by-feasibility score from the earlier methodology is penalised by tier so that a top-tier use case needs more value to rank. The per-tier controls below are drawn from Canada Appendix C, MAS, EU Articles 9–15, ISO 42001 Annex A and the OWASP agentic ladder; the goal is that the client can see, for each recommendation, exactly what compliance work it buys.

| Tier | Trigger (score or override) | Required controls before go-live | Validation and review | Decision authority |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T0 Prohibited | EU Art 5 practices; China/Vietnam prohibited acts; nudification; social scoring; subliminal manipulation; real-time public biometric ID without legal basis | None; use case rejected | n/a | Rejected, logged with reason |
| T1 Low | Score 0–5; internal productivity, no personal data, human edits all output | Inventory record; acceptable-use policy; AI-use disclosure to staff; vendor on approved list with retention terms | Peer review; annual re-check | Use-case owner |
| T2 Moderate | Score 6–10; personal data or customer-facing content with human review before release | T1 plus DPIA/PIA; impact assessment (ISO A.5); output disclaimers and AI-interaction disclosure; hallucination and data-leakage testing per IMDA Starter Kit; logging of prompts, model version and reviewer | Peer review plus documented test results; biennial review; quarterly KRIs | Function head with risk sign-off |
| T3 High | Score 11–15, or override: regulated eligibility, health, financial advice (SFC), minors, EU Annex III domain, Korea high-impact | T2 plus independent validation (MAS), fairness and robustness testing, Annex IV-style technical file, human-in-the-loop with meaningful explanation per decision, contingency and rollback plan, six-month decision reconstruction, incident timelines, in-context testing of third-party models | Independent expert review; annual re-validation; monthly monitoring | Governance committee with recorded risk acceptance |
| T4 Critical | Score 16+, or override: employment screening, credit decisioning, autonomous production actions, irreversible external actions, cross-border sensitive data at scale | T3 plus two independent reviews, publication of assessment summary where required, red-team pre-deployment, kill switch tested on schedule, executive risk acceptance, regulator notification where applicable (SFC, MAS material change), contractual indemnity and audit rights with vendors | Independent re-validation per cycle; continuous monitoring | Named executive and board committee |

Agentic use cases need a second gate layered on the tier, because the tier captures the decision and the agent gate captures the action. The convergent control stack across OWASP ASI01–ASI10, CSA MAESTRO, IMDA's agentic framework, NIST's agent initiative and MAS is now clear: unique agent identity with short-lived task-scoped credentials and no reuse of human credentials, least-privilege tool allowlists, approval tiers by reversibility, immutable action logs capturing instructions, retrieved context, tool calls, parameters, results and human approvals, sandboxed execution with deny-by-default egress, blast-radius isolation and circuit breakers, an owner and expiry per agent, and a kill switch that is tested rather than assumed ([Cycode](https://cycode.com/blog/owasp-top-10-agentic-applications/), [CSA on NIST AASI](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-nist-ai-agent-standards-initiative-complia/), [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/04/singapores-agentic-ai-framework-practical-guidance-for-market-entry)). AWS's Agentic Lens encodes the approval tiers as Autonomous (low risk, reversible, execute), Notify (medium, proceed and inform) and Approve (high-impact or irreversible, explicit human approval carrying the action description, agent reasoning, impact assessment and execution history, with timeouts that fail safe) ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/agentic-ai-lens/agentrel02-bp05.html)). OWASP's maturity model adds the rule that any session combining private data access, untrusted content and external communication requires human approval ([CSA note](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-owasp-agentic-ai-governance-maturity-v2-20/)). IMDA's framework grades agent risk by domain error tolerance, access to sensitive data, reversibility, task complexity, scope of actions and autonomy level, and asks deployers to monitor human override rates and response times as an automation-bias check ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/04/singapores-agentic-ai-framework-practical-guidance-for-market-entry), [Baker McKenzie](https://www.bakermckenzie.com/en/insight/publications/2026/06/singapore-imda-updates-model-ai-governance-framework-for-agentic-ai)). The agent gate should therefore classify each tool the agent can call as reversible (read, draft), reviewable (propose changes) or irreversible (delete, move funds, rotate secrets, send externally), place the use case on OWASP's adoption tiers AT0–AT8, and refuse autonomous operation for any irreversible tool above T1 ([NHI Management Group](https://nhimg.org/glossary/action-risk-tiering/)). This gives the firm an external citation anchor for its own reversibility vocabulary.

The go/no-go rules at the end of the gate should be explicit and few. A use case proceeds to the recommendation deck only if it has a named accountable owner, a tier with its control checklist costed into the business case, no unresolved T0 flag, a data-rights and residency basis for every data source and model route, a baseline and measurement plan, and, for T3 and above, a validation path that does not depend solely on vendor attestations. It is conditioned if the controls exist on paper but not in evidence, escalated if an override fires on a use case the client has already deployed, and rejected if the jurisdiction map shows a binding prohibition or a filing regime the client cannot satisfy (the China case, where a Singapore firm should gate on local-partner filing capability rather than attempt direct compliance). Because Gartner expects over 40% of agentic projects to be cancelled partly for inadequate risk controls and BCG finds only 5% of firms hold the full control set for agents with decision authority while 42% expect to grant it by 2030, agent governance should be presented as a built-in "avoid risk" value pool in nearly every engagement rather than a tax on the "make money" pools ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027), [BCG](https://www.bcg.com/press/30september2026-ai-starting-to-pay-off-companies-generate-value)).

## Leave behind an evidence pack auditors can sample

The recurring principle from Schellman, SecurityScientist and GAICC is that each requirement needs a named owner and a sampled evidence artefact; as SecurityScientist puts it, a requirement with no evidence is not met ([SecurityScientist](https://www.securityscientist.net/blog/eu-ai-act-high-risk-requirements-checklist-template/)). The commercial insight is that the documents an ISO 42001 auditor, an EU notified body or a MAS supervisor will ask for are largely the same documents the engagement must produce anyway to justify its recommendations, and that the two controls GRC platforms automate worst, the impact assessment and the inventory, are exactly the ones a consulting engagement produces by hand ([aiactindex.eu](https://aiactindex.eu/reviews/vanta)). ISO 42001's mandatory documents by clause are the scope (4.3), AI policy (5.2), risk register and methodology (6.1.1–6.1.2), Statement of Applicability (6.1.3), AI system impact assessment (6.1.4), objectives (6.2), competence evidence (7.2), monitoring report (9.1), internal audit (9.2), management review (9.3) and corrective action (10.2), plus Annex A artefacts such as the resource register, impact methodology and results, lifecycle requirements, verification and validation, technical documentation, event logs, data provenance records, user information, incident communication plan and supplier responsibility split ([Advisera](https://advisera.com/articles/iso-42001-mandatory-documents/), [GAICC](https://gaicc.org/blog/iso-42001-annex-a-controls/)). EU high-risk evidence runs Articles 9 through 17 plus the conformity declaration, registration, post-market monitoring and deployer oversight records ([SecurityScientist](https://www.securityscientist.net/blog/eu-ai-act-high-risk-requirements-checklist-template/)). KPMG's own controls guide adds model and system cards, a CMDB, TEVV documentation, ten-year retention of AI documentation, documented override and pause scenarios, exit strategies and quarterly KPI/KRI review by the system owner ([KPMG PDF](https://kpmg.com/kpmg-us/content/dam/kpmg/pdf/2025/trusted-ai-controls-matrix-tool-us.pdf), [KPMG system cards](https://kpmg.com/us/en/articles/2025/when-trust-ai-matters.html)).

The evidence pack should therefore consist of ten artefacts, each produced in the client's own template so it drops directly into a certification or supervisory file, each carrying an owner, a version, a retention rule and a pointer to the standard clauses it satisfies.

| # | Artefact | Produced in which engagement step | Satisfies |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | AI inventory record per use case (MAS fields plus tier) | Maturity and use-case steps | MAS AIRG s.4; ISO A.4, A.6.2.7; AU DTA register; Korea dossier |
| 2 | Impact assessment (Canada AIA-style score, ISO A.5 individual and societal sections, DPIA where personal data, FRIA where EU-exposed) | Gate | ISO 6.1.4/42005; PDPA; EU Art 27; Korea best-efforts FRIA |
| 3 | Risk register entry with tier, treatment and residual risk against appetite | Gate | ISO 6.1; MAS risk appetite; EU Art 9 |
| 4 | Control checklist per tier with owner and evidence pointer | Gate | ISO SoA; EN 18286 Art 17 elements; AICM objectives |
| 5 | Test records (fairness, robustness, hallucination, data leakage, red-team) and release criteria | Use-case validation | ISO A.6.2.4; MAS testing; IMDA Starter Kit; EU Art 15; Microsoft A3 pattern |
| 6 | Logging and human-oversight design incl. override and kill-switch procedure | Use-case design | EU Arts 12, 14; ISO A.6.2.8; IMDA agentic; MAS agentic expectations |
| 7 | Supplier and LLM-vendor assessment with residency, retention and audit-rights terms | Research and gate | ISO A.10; SOC 2 subservice treatment per TQA 9561; MAS third-party AI; EU Art 25 |
| 8 | Monitoring plan with KPI/KRI cadence, drift thresholds and decision-reconstruction capability | Roadmap | ISO 9.1; MAS monitoring; EU post-market |

Two refinements make the pack more valuable than a document dump. First, a tiered public-facing summary modelled on the UK's Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard, with a Tier 1 plain-language record and a Tier 2 technical and governance disclosure, gives public-sector and consumer-facing clients a ready artefact for the Australian transparency-statement regime, where 30 of 92 agencies missed the accountable-official deadline and more than half missed the statement deadline ([Burden of Proof](https://theburdenofproof.substack.com/p/show-your-workings-what-the-uks-atrs), [ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-06-12/government-agencies-miss-ai-transparency-test/106789344)). Second, the pack should state which artefacts a certification body would sample at Stage 2 and which a MAS supervisor would expect at the 2027 and 2028 milestones, so that the client can treat the engagement as the first third of a certification or readiness programme; Schellman's readiness indicators are leadership commitment, a fully implemented AIMS, operational risk and compliance measures, completed internal audits and readily available system documentation ([Schellman](https://schellman.com/blog/iso-certifications/iso-42001-lessons-learned)).

## Attest the firm's own tool before selling assurance

KPMG's reasoning for certifying itself, that you cannot guide clients on a journey you are not on, is now the market norm: KPMG Australia was certified first in October 2024, KPMG US in November 2025, KPMG International in December 2025 and KPMG Canada in September 2026, with BCG among the first 100 certified organisations in January 2026, and KPMG's agentic platform aIQ Capture obtained AIUC-1 certification after more than 900 tests including hallucination and prompt injection ([KPMG International](https://kpmg.com/xx/en/media/press-releases/2025/12/kpmg-international-first-to-attain-iso-certification-for-ai-management-systems.html), [BCG](https://www.bcg.com/news/27january2026-bcg-certified-international-standard-ai-management-systems), [KPMG AIUC-1](https://kpmg.com/us/en/media/news/kpmg-llp-becomes-first-big-four-firm-with-aiuc-1-certified-ai-capability.html)). For a small firm the costs and timelines are now visible. SOC 2 Type II for a small AI SaaS runs roughly **US$25,000–80,000** in year one, with auditor fees of US$15,000–45,000, a 3–12 month observation window and a realistic six to nine months end to end ([Devbrows](https://www.devbrows.com/blog/soc2-type-2-cost-timeline-saas-2026)). ISO 42001 year-one totals are about **US$73,000 for a 30-person firm** using third-party AI tools, with certification-body fees of US$20,000–40,000 at Schellman or US$25,000–50,000 at BSI, 200–400 internal hours, surveillance at 30–40% of initial cost, a typical six to twelve months, and a documented example of an organisation already holding ISO 27001 and 9001 paying around US$35,000 to add 42001 against an estimated US$70,000–90,000 without ([CertBetter](https://certbetter.com/blog/iso-42001-cost-what-ai-certification-actually-costs-in-2026), [Modalpoint](https://modalpoint.com/blog/ai-governance-compliance-landscape/)). Vanta's ISO 42001 module costs roughly €6,000–14,000 a year on top of base pricing and maps Annex A to SOC 2 and ISO 27001 evidence, which is why the sequencing should be SOC 2 and ISO 27001 first, 42001 second: ISO 27001 holders reuse roughly half the management-system work, and Stage 2 audits are one to three weeks against a three-year certificate cycle ([aiactindex.eu](https://aiactindex.eu/reviews/vanta), [ITSECOPS](https://itsecops.cloud/compliance/iso-42001/), [Schellman](https://schellman.com/blog/iso-certifications/iso-42001-lessons-learned)). In Singapore the relevant accredited body is TÜV SÜD PSB under SAC; in Australia, Intertek SAI Global under JAS-ANZ ([Atoro](https://atoro.io/iso-42001-certification-bodies/)). The EDGE grant reportedly requires an SS 680-certified consultant for ISO 42001 projects, which the earlier research flagged as unconfirmed across sources and which should be verified with Enterprise Singapore before it is used in positioning ([Twenty Eight Eleven](https://twentyeighteleven.sg/blog/edge-grant-ai-projects-singapore)).

The SOC 2 gap matters more for this product than for most SaaS, because SOC 2 does not natively test accuracy, provenance or bias, and the firm's core risk is a fabricated citation reaching a client. CSA's August 2026 analysis finds SOC 2 covers infrastructure controls but not training-data provenance, evaluation methodology, bias, explainability, accuracy or drift, and recommends per-inference logging of model version, prompt hash, tool calls and outcome, model change management with reversible deployments, documentation of each LLM vendor as a subservice organisation with its retention configuration, and agent connector allowlists, paired with the CSA AI Controls Matrix v1.1 (247 objectives, 18 domains) and a combined SOC 2 plus AICM attestation under STAR for AI ([CSA PDF](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/CSA_research_note_soc2_ai_controls_gap_20260830-csa-styled.pdf)). The nine artefacts auditors sample first for LLM applications are a model registry with immutable history, per-call logging, tamper-evident retention, vendor DPAs with training opt-out or zero-data-retention clauses, drift and regression alert history, bias reports, tested rollback, secrets management with rotation, and tool-level permissioning for agents ([MLflow](https://mlflow.org/articles/soc-2-for-llm-apps/)). Citation verification should be written as an explicit Processing Integrity control objective with testable criteria: every citation in a deliverable resolves to a fetched source stored with a hash, every quotation string-matches the stored source, every load-bearing number has two independent upstreams, unverifiable claims are blocked from the deliverable rather than flagged, aggregator-only sources are traced to the original, and AI-generated pages are rejected; the earlier research found deep-research agents hallucinate 3–13% of URLs and that only 24–77% of citations support the attributed fact, so this control is where the firm's quality claim lives or dies. OWASP's LLM01 guidance adds the input-side controls: threat-model every input channel (retrieved chunks, tool outputs, documents, OCR) with a documented detection control and measured false-positive and false-negative rates, keep high-impact tools unreachable from untrusted indirect input without a human gate, log per-input scores for at least 90 days and make every output traceable to a retrieved chunk or tool call ([promptinjectionprevention.com](https://www.promptinjectionprevention.com/kb/owasp-llm-top-10-2025.php)). The research agent itself should be gated by the same reversibility rules it recommends to clients: searches and reads autonomous, draft outputs notify, any send, publish or write to a client system requires approval, and the lethal-trifecta rule applies because the agent handles client data, reads untrusted web content and can communicate externally.

LLM vendor data terms are now a residency and retention question that can be answered precisely. OpenAI has offered data residency for API projects and enterprise workspaces in Singapore, Japan, Korea, India and Australia since 25 November 2025, with in-region handling, no storage at rest for API requests and no training on business data by default, holding SOC 2 Type 2 and ISO 27001/27017/27018/27701 but not listing ISO 42001 ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/expanding-data-residency-access-to-business-customers-worldwide/)). Anthropic's commercial products hold SOC 2 Type I and II, ISO 27001:2022 and ISO/IEC 42001:2023, but zero-data-retention requires application and approval, default API retention is 30 days, and The Register reported in September 2026 that customers must verify ZDR actually applied, with a customer-controlled "Enterprise Frontier Safeguards" option planned for autumn 2026 ([Anthropic](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/10015870-what-certifications-has-anthropic-obtained), [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/09/02/anthropic-promises-zero-data-retention-but-customers-must-check-it-worked/5293789)). On Google Cloud, the Interactions API stores by default unless set otherwise, abuse-monitoring logs persist up to 90 days on the standard tier, global endpoints carry no residency guarantee and are the default unless pinned, and partner Claude models retain prompts up to 30 days with sharing to Anthropic as a condition ([IntuitionLabs](https://intuitionlabs.ai/insights/guides/zero-data-retention-gemini-vertex-ai-life-sciences)). The operational rules are therefore to pin traffic to Singapore endpoints, obtain ZDR or equivalent approval in writing and test it, record each vendor as a subservice organisation with its retention configuration per TQA 9561, document the PDPA section 26 transfer basis for any non-Singapore route, prefer vendors that have signed the EU GPAI Code of Practice for EU-exposed work (Meta and the Chinese labs have not), and note the vendor's EU establishment given the Garante annulment ([Arthur AI](https://www.arthur.ai/column/soc-2-ai-agents-aicpa-tqa-9561), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General-Purpose_AI_Code_of_Practice)). Finally, every deliverable should carry an AI-use disclosure stating that it is AI-assisted and human-reviewed, naming the models and tools used and the verification steps taken, because the APS AI Plan requires RFQ-stage disclosure and full provider responsibility, California requires generative-AI disclosure language in contracts, and the EU Article 50 exemption for AI-generated text depends on human editorial responsibility ([Canberra Times](https://www.canberratimes.com.au/story/9110516/new-ai-plan-for-the-aps-sets-out-expectations-for-contractors/), [GenAI.ca.gov](https://www.genai.ca.gov/choose-your-journey/procure-tool/follow-procurement-process/disclosure-contract-language/), [Cookiebot](https://www.cookiebot.com/en/eu-ai-act-article-50-transparency-compliance/)).

## One AICM-keyed control library crosswalks everything

Published crosswalks are mostly qualitative, and the firm should resist quoting a precise overlap percentage. The ISO42k editorial mapping rates nine of the nineteen NIST AI RMF categories as strongly covered by ISO 42001 and ten as partial with zero outright gaps, noting that 42001 leaves testing methods to the organisation, lacks the twelve generative-AI risks and treats trustworthiness characteristics as informative only, while the RMF has no certification scheme ([ISO42k](https://www.iso42k.com/nist-ai-rmf-and-iso-42001)). The only numeric SOC 2 to 42001 figure, 38 of 61 controls or 62% with a candidate mapping, comes from an unreviewed automated tool ([The Art of Service](https://compliance.theartofservice.com/compare/soc-2-vs-iso-iec-42001-2023)). CSA's April 2026 analysis of prEN 18286 against ISO 42001 identifies five EU AI Act areas 42001 does not fully address, namely per-system compliance strategy, change management for continuously learning systems, Article 73 incident timelines, AI-specific supply-chain provisions and the Article 27 fundamental-rights assessment, and recommends a layered architecture of 42001 for organisational governance, EN 18286 for per-system QMS and AICM for technical controls ([CSA](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-eu-ai-act-pren-18286-iso-42001-20260428-cs/)). AICM itself maps control by control to ISO 42001, NIST AI 600-1, BSI AIC4 and the EU AI Act, with ISO 27001/27002 compensating controls for partial gaps, and underpins STAR for AI, whose provisional Level 2 is built on an ISO 42001 certificate plus a scored AI-CAIQ ([CSA](https://cloudsecurityalliance.org/blog/2025/08/20/announcing-the-ai-controls-matrix-and-iso-iec-42001-mapping-and-the-roadmap-to-star-for-ai-42001)). HITRUST's AI controls are harmonised to NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 23894 and the OWASP LLM Top 10; OWASP's 2026 list cross-references NIST, ATLAS and CWE; and FINOS v2.0 cross-references seven frameworks ([HITRUST](https://hitrustalliance.net/assessments-and-certifications/airiskmanagementassessment), [FINOS](https://www.finos.org/blog/finos-ai-governance-framework-v2.0-addressing-agentic-ai-risks-in-a-rapidly-evolving-landscape)).

The economical design is a single control library keyed to AICM v1.1's 247 objectives, with mapping columns to ISO 42001 Annex A (38 controls), NIST AI RMF (72 subcategories) and AI 600-1 risks, EN 18286 Article 17 elements (13), SOC 2 trust services criteria, OWASP LLM 2026 and ASI identifiers, MITRE ATLAS technique families, AI Verify's 106 process checks, MAS AIRG sections and the IMDA agentic framework's four dimensions. Each control carries a status flag inherited from the status map so that draft-standard controls (COSIS overlays, 42105, 27091) appear as direction-of-travel annotations rather than scored items, and each carries the sector overlays where they exist: ISO/IEC 27701:2025's automated-decision expectations for privacy, SFC and MAS for finance, AIHGle 2.0 and HSA SaMD for health, ISO/PAS 8800 for automotive ([Linford & Co](https://linfordco.com/?p=23924), [SGS](https://www.sgs.com/news/2025/04/safeguards-04625-introducing-iso-pas-8800-functional-safety-for-ai-in-road-vehicles)). One evidence item satisfies every framework it maps to, which is what lets the engagement claim that its evidence pack serves 42001 certification, STAR for AI, SOC 2 AI evidence, MAS readiness and EU technical controls at once. The library must be re-synced at least quarterly: ATLAS now ships monthly, OWASP shipped a new LLM list in August 2026 and an Agent Control Standard v0.1 in September, the NIST RMF revision will change subcategory vocabulary, and the MAS final text, the Malaysian and Thai bills and Australia's standards will each add rows within twelve months ([CSA](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-owasp-genai-top10-2026-agent-control-stand/)). Vocabulary should be kept in its own layer so that re-mapping is a column change, not a rewrite.

## Position inside the government-seeded assurance market, then build in four waves

Governments are deliberately seeding third-party AI assurance markets, and Singapore is the most advanced in Asia. The UK sized its assurance sector at 524 firms, £1.01 billion revenue and 12,572 jobs in November 2024 with a projection of £6.53 billion by 2035, and its September 2025 roadmap funds a professionalisation consortium, a skills framework and an £11 million innovation fund, explicitly referencing Singapore's AI Verify Foundation ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/ensuring-trust-in-ai-to-unlock-65-billion-over-next-decade), [AIGL](https://www.aigl.blog/trusted-third-party-ai-assurance-roadmap-uk-government-dsit-september-2025/)). Singapore's Global AI Assurance Pilot paired 17 deployers with 16 testing firms across 10 industries, found that off-the-shelf benchmarks were rarely useful and that participants wanted accreditation and standards, which IMDA delivered on 14 October 2026 by accrediting AIDX Tech, Knovel Engineering, PwC Singapore, Resaro and Vulcan under AI TAP, by method (benchmarking, red-teaming) across five risk areas, while launching an agentic testing starter kit and the FAIRS taskforce with guidelines due in the first half of 2027 ([AI Verify Foundation](https://aiverifyfoundation.sg/ai-assurance/2025-main-report/executive-summary/), [LittleBigRedDot](https://littlebigreddot.com/imda-ai-agent-testing-kit-accredited-ai-testers-fairs-2026/)). AI TAP recognises competence only, not independence or system safety, which leaves room for a firm that pairs accredited testing with strategy. The regional demand drivers are specific: MAS's expectation of independent evidence for third-party AI makes accredited testing a procurement input for every FI by 2028; Australia's DTA policy requires impact assessments, registers and transparency statements that agencies will ask vendors to help produce by 30 April 2027; Korea's public bodies must prefer products with completed impact assessments; and APAC readiness for AI regulation sits at 12% in Schellman's survey ([ACS](https://membership.acs.org.au/member-insight/2025-12-9-Version-2-0-Policy-for-the-Responsible-Use-of-AI-in-Government,.html), [Pebblous](https://blog.pebblous.ai/report/korea-ai-basic-act-highimpact-governance/en/)). The positioning that follows is "AI-accelerated, human-accountable, source-verified, and accredited to test", sold to three buyers: FIs facing the MAS clock, Australian agencies and their suppliers facing the DTA and Privacy Act clocks, and regional enterprises selling into Korea, Vietnam or the EU that need a tiered classification they can defend.

The build should run in four waves over roughly eighteen months, sequenced so that each wave produces something billable and something the next wave needs. Wave one (months 0–4) builds the status map, the five-tier gate with overrides and per-tier controls, the agent gate, the regulatory-exposure matrix and the ten-artefact evidence pack as templates, keys the control library to AICM v1.1 with the mapping columns above, writes the citation-verification and AI-use-disclosure controls into the tool's own design, pins LLM traffic to Singapore endpoints with ZDR confirmed in writing, and begins SOC 2 scoping and readiness, which can be done alongside ISO 27001 since the evidence base overlaps. This wave lets the firm sell the compliance dimension and the tier gate inside its two-to-four-week assessment immediately, and offer MAS AIRG gap assessments to FIs ahead of the October 2027 date. Wave two (months 4–10) completes the SOC 2 Type II observation window and ISO 27001 certification, launches the compliance sub-dimension in the free self-scored benchmark to start accumulating peer data, and applies for AI TAP accreditation in at least one method, most plausibly benchmarking using Project Moonshot and the IMDA LLM and agentic starter kits, since AI Verify is already crosswalked to NIST AI RMF, ISO 42001 and AI 600-1 ([Chambers](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/artificial-intelligence-2026/singapore)). Wave three (months 10–16) adds ISO 42001 on top of 27001 with TÜV SÜD PSB or another accredited body, publishes the firm's own AI inventory, impact assessment and transparency statement as a worked example of the evidence pack, and posts a STAR for AI AI-CAIQ so the firm appears where vendor-risk teams look. Wave four (months 16–18 and ongoing) adds red-teaming accreditation, extends the exposure map to the Malaysian and Thai statutes and Australian standards as they pass, and builds the MAS 2028 lifecycle-controls offer, by which time the firm holds the credentials that let it sit on both sides of the assurance market: the advisor who designs the controls and the accredited tester who evidences them. Total external cost across the four waves, using the mid-points above, is on the order of US$150,000–250,000 over eighteen months plus internal time, which is less than the Big 4 price anchor for a single assessment and buys a position almost no regional boutique holds.

## Conclusion

The compliance layer changes the economics of the engagement in a way the earlier methodology only hinted at. Because regulators across APJC have chosen disclosure, contractual rights and in-context testing over named certifications, and because the dated obligations fall in a narrow window from December 2026 to October 2028, every client in the firm's target market has a compliance programme it has not yet started, and the maturity assessment, the tier gate and the evidence pack are that programme's first deliverables. Deloitte's finding that risk-only investment does not correlate with value while trust-building does is the design brief: the layer must be presented as the thing that makes the "make money" use cases deployable and the agentic "save money" use cases survivable, not as a control function that slows them down.

The less obvious implication is that the firm's own attestation stack and its product are the same artefact. The research agent that assesses a client's third-party AI controls is itself third-party AI the client must assess, and a citation-verification control written as a Processing Integrity objective, an AI-use disclosure on every deliverable, and an ISO 42001 certificate are not overhead but the demonstration that the method works. Singapore's decision to accredit testers by method rather than to license assurance firms leaves a gap between accredited testing and strategic advice that a small firm can occupy before the incumbents, who are now certifying themselves one entity at a time, close it.
